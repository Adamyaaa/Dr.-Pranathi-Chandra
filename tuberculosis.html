Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis is a bacterial infection that most commonly affects the lungs but can involve lymph nodes, bones, the abdomen and other sites. It is a treatable condition. The two things that determine the outcome are starting the correct treatment early, and completing the full course.

Symptoms that should be assessed

  • Cough lasting more than two to three weeks
  • Fever, characteristically rising in the evening
  • Night sweats
  • Losing weight without trying, and loss of appetite
  • Blood in the phlegm
  • Chest pain, or persistent tiredness
  • A swelling in the neck that has not settled

None of these on its own means tuberculosis. Equally, none of them should be treated with repeated courses of antibiotics or cough syrup without an assessment.

If you have had TB treatment before

Say so at the first consultation, and bring the old prescriptions and reports if you have them. Previous treatment — especially a course that was stopped early — changes which tests are needed and which drugs are appropriate. This is one of the most important pieces of information you can give.

Tests used to confirm it

  • Sputum examination, including rapid molecular tests such as CBNAAT/NAAT, which detect the organism and screen for resistance to rifampicin
  • Chest X-ray, and a CT scan of the chest where the picture is unclear
  • Culture and drug-sensitivity testing, which guides treatment when resistance is suspected
  • Tissue or fluid sampling where the disease is outside the lungs
  • Baseline blood tests, including liver function, before treatment begins

Treatment and follow-up

Treatment uses a combination of medicines over several months, in a regimen decided by the site of disease, the sensitivity results and your treatment history. Follow-up during that period is not optional — it exists to confirm the disease is responding, to pick up side effects such as liver irritation early, and to adjust treatment if the results change.

Stopping early is the main cause of drug-resistant tuberculosis. People often feel considerably better within a few weeks, well before the infection has been cleared. Finishing the prescribed course, at the prescribed doses, is what prevents relapse and resistance.

About the people around you

Household contacts, particularly young children, elderly relatives and anyone with reduced immunity, should be screened. This is arranged as part of the consultation. Tuberculosis treatment is available free of charge through India's national TB programme, and treatment in the private sector is notifiable to it — so you can be treated here and remain within that system.

When not to wait for an appointment

Coughing a large amount of blood, sudden severe breathlessness or chest pain needs emergency care, not an appointment request.
