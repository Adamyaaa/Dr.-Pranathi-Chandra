COPD

COPD — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease — is long-term narrowing of the airways that does not fully reverse. It develops slowly, usually after years of exposure to tobacco smoke, cooking-fire smoke, or dust and fumes at work. Treatment aims to reduce symptoms, prevent flare-ups and slow further loss of lung function.

What it usually feels like

  • Breathlessness that has crept up over years, first on exertion, later on ordinary activity
  • A cough most days, often bringing up phlegm, particularly in the mornings
  • Repeated chest infections, especially in winter
  • Wheeze and a feeling of tightness
  • Tiredness, and doing less than you used to without quite deciding to

Many people put these down to age or to being unfit. That is the main reason COPD is often picked up late, once a good deal of lung function has already been lost.

An important local point about exposure

COPD is not only a smoker's disease. In India, long-term exposure to smoke from firewood and chulha cooking is a well-recognised cause, and it affects women who have never smoked a cigarette in their lives. Occupational dust — stone, cement, textiles, welding fumes — matters too. Please mention these at the consultation even if you have never smoked.

How it is diagnosed

Spirometry is essential. COPD cannot be confirmed on symptoms or an X-ray alone. The breathing test measures airflow obstruction and how much of it persists after a bronchodilator, which is what distinguishes COPD from asthma. A chest X-ray, blood tests and oxygen saturation are usually done alongside.

What changes the course of the illness

Stopping smoking is the only intervention shown to slow the ongoing decline in lung function. No inhaler does that. Everything else — inhalers, vaccination, pulmonary rehabilitation — improves how you feel and reduces flare-ups, which matters a great deal, but stopping exposure is the part that alters the trajectory. Support to stop is part of the consultation, not a lecture at the end of it.

What management involves

  • Inhaled treatment matched to symptom burden and flare-up history, with technique checked at every review
  • Influenza and pneumococcal vaccination
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation — structured exercise and breathing training, which reliably improves exercise capacity
  • A written plan for flare-ups, so treatment starts early rather than after a week of waiting
  • Assessment for long-term oxygen where blood oxygen levels are persistently low

When not to wait for an appointment

Seek urgent care if breathlessness worsens sharply over hours or days, if the phlegm changes colour and you develop fever, if you become confused or unusually drowsy, or if your lips or fingertips look blue.
