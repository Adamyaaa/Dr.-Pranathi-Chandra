Sleep-related breathing problems

Loud snoring with pauses in breathing is not simply a nuisance to the person in the next room. In obstructive sleep apnoea the airway closes repeatedly during sleep, oxygen levels dip, and sleep is fragmented dozens of times an hour without the person remembering any of it. It is strongly linked to high blood pressure, diabetes, heart rhythm problems and road accidents.

Signs worth taking seriously

  • Loud, habitual snoring
  • Someone has noticed you stop breathing, then gasp or snort
  • Waking unrefreshed however long you slept
  • Sleepiness during the day — in meetings, watching television, and most seriously while driving
  • Morning headache, dry mouth, waking to pass urine several times
  • Difficulty concentrating, irritability, low mood
  • Blood pressure that stays high despite several medicines

Bring the person who sleeps in the room

You cannot describe your own sleep. A partner or family member can tell in thirty seconds what would otherwise take a long consultation to guess at — how loud the snoring is, whether breathing stops, how long the pauses last, and how restless the night is. If they can come along, it genuinely helps.

How it is assessed

The consultation covers sleep pattern, snoring history, daytime sleepiness scoring, weight and neck measurement, an examination of the nose and throat, and a review of blood pressure, sugar and thyroid status. Where the picture suggests sleep apnoea, a sleep study is arranged — an overnight recording of breathing, oxygen levels, heart rate and effort. This can often be done at home rather than in a laboratory.

What treatment involves

  • CPAP — a device that keeps the airway open with gently pressurised air. It is the standard treatment for moderate and severe obstructive sleep apnoea, and it works from the first night when the mask fits and the pressure is right. Getting those two things right is most of the job.
  • Weight reduction, which meaningfully reduces severity and, in milder cases, can resolve it
  • Positional measures, where events occur mainly while sleeping on the back
  • Treating nasal blockage, which otherwise makes any device hard to tolerate
  • Oral appliances or an ENT opinion in selected cases

A word about driving

Untreated sleep apnoea with significant daytime sleepiness raises the risk of falling asleep at the wheel. If you drive for a living, or drive long distances, please say so at the consultation. It changes how urgently the assessment is arranged.
