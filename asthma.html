Asthma

Asthma is a long-term condition in which the airways become inflamed and narrow in response to triggers. It is managed rather than cured, and most people who are on the right treatment, used correctly, live an ordinary life without restriction.

What it usually feels like

  • Wheezing — a whistling sound in the chest, often worse at night or in the early morning
  • Cough that keeps returning, sometimes as the only symptom
  • Breathlessness while walking fast, climbing stairs or exercising
  • Tightness in the chest
  • Symptoms triggered by dust, smoke, cold air, strong smells, pets, pollen or a viral cold

Why the first visit is mostly questions

Asthma is diagnosed largely on the pattern of symptoms — what brings them on, what time of day they are worst, how they respond to an inhaler, and whether there is a personal or family history of allergy or eczema. The examination and tests confirm the picture rather than create it. So the first consultation involves a fairly detailed history.

Tests that may be used

  • Spirometry — a breathing test that measures how much air you can blow out and how fast. Often repeated after a dose of a reliever medicine to see whether the airways open up.
  • Peak flow monitoring — a simple home device, used over days or weeks to show variation.
  • Chest X-ray — usually to rule out other causes rather than to diagnose asthma itself.
  • Allergy assessment — where there is a clear seasonal or trigger-related pattern.

The single most useful thing you can do

Bring your inhaler to the appointment. A large share of "asthma that isn't controlled" is in fact asthma treated with a correct medicine that is not reaching the lungs. Technique is checked in the room, with your own device, and corrected on the spot. It costs nothing and often changes more than a new prescription would.

What long-term control involves

Treatment is usually built around a preventer inhaler taken every day, whether or not you feel unwell, plus a reliever for symptoms. The aim is that the reliever is rarely needed. Needing it frequently is a signal that the control plan needs review, not a signal to simply use more of it.

Reviews also cover trigger management at home and at work, vaccination where appropriate, and a written plan for what to do when symptoms flare.

When not to wait for an appointment

Go to the nearest emergency department if you are too breathless to complete a sentence, if the reliever inhaler is not helping or is needed again within a couple of hours, or if your lips or fingertips look blue.
